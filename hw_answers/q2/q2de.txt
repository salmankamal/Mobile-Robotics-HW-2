e)

i)
The physics engine inside Gazebo does not step synchronously with computer's clock time; it steps forward in discrete numerical integration intervals dictated by simulation time (/clock). Because the node command velocities are for precise durations, the durations must be measured in the exact time domain that the physics engine integrates. If the simulation runs at half of real-time and the node relies on computer's clock time, 1 second of the computer's clock time corresponds to only 0.5 seconds of simulated physics time. The robot would only move for half the required duration in the virtual world, driving only half the side length and rotating through half the turn angle.  

ii)
As discussed in our class, the velocity command steps instantaneously from 0 to v or omega (for angular velocity), demanding infinite acceleration which is not possible. The simulated physics engine enforces wheel friction and inertia, resulting in brief slip during starts and skidding during turn stops rather than pure rolling without slipping (which is our assumptions for the kinematic model we made). The command loop runs at a discrete rate. A phase transition from straight line to rotation may overshoot or undershoot the ideal boundary, accumulating small angular errors that compound over four turns.
